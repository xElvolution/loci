# loci and Qloo: the problem

## Coming home is the hardest part of a hospital stay

Every year millions of people leave hospital with a printed discharge letter, a bag of medicines and a follow-up date. The first weeks at home decide a lot: whether medicines are taken on time, whether warning signs are noticed early, whether the person keeps moving, sleeps and eats, and whether they feel like themselves again.

This is true for every specialty, not one niche:

- a new mother after a cesarean, juggling her own recovery and a newborn
- someone two weeks out from a knee replacement, doing exercises three times a day
- a heart patient learning a new medicine schedule
- a stroke survivor relearning daily tasks with a partner who has become a carer overnight
- a parent bringing a child home after surgery
- a teenager or adult leaving a mental health stay
- a person between chemotherapy cycles
- an older parent whose daughter lives in another city

## What goes wrong

1. **The plan lives on paper.** Instructions are scattered across a letter, a pharmacy label and a physio sheet. Times are easy to miss and warning signs are forgotten.
2. **The people helping are not in step.** Family, friends and paid carers each know a piece. Nobody knows who is picking up the prescription.
3. **Care teams go blind after discharge.** Clinics and home care agencies have little signal between visits and no simple way to see who needs a call.
4. **Recovery days are long and flat.** Generic wellness apps offer the same playlist and recipes to everyone. People recover better when the day feels like theirs: their music, their shows, food they actually like, a gentle outing they would choose.

## Why existing tools fall short

- Hospital portals are built for records, not daily life at home, and rarely include family.
- Medication reminder apps cover one slice and ignore the circle around the patient.
- Wellness apps personalize nothing that matters and often blur into medical advice.
- Remote monitoring products are expensive, specialty-specific and clinical.
- Nothing connects the two sides: the team writes instructions once on paper and never sees whether they were followed, and the family has no easy way to ask or to learn the plan changed.

## The opportunity

A two-sided, privacy-first loop that works for any specialty: the care team writes and adjusts the plan, the patient and family follow it step by step, and the team sees progress live. It keeps the plan in the care team's own words, brings the circle together, gives clinics a simple signal, and uses taste intelligence to make each recovery day feel personal, without ever pretending to be a clinician.
